### CONS-1 — Presale/financing model makes condo supply hostage to rates and investor demand
- Mechanism: Lenders require ~70% presales [per press; unverified against lender terms] before construction financing. Higher rates cut investor buyer pro formas (negative carry, cap rates) and raise carry/construction-loan costs, so presales fall, projects cannot launch, and starts collapse with a 3–4-year lag; supply is pro-cyclical and fragile.
- Predicted effect: Negative on condo starts/completions when rates rise; magnitude in GTHA 2025: condo starts -63%, sales -60%. Completions will fall sharply 2027–29 (forecast, not measured).
- Unit of analysis: Project (launch/cancel/start), CMA-quarter.
- Required variables: presale %, price/sf, loan terms, rates, investor share, assignment/closing defaults, start/cancel dates.
- Potential datasets: Urbanation, CMHC Starts & Completions Survey, BoC rates, Altus/Teranet, court receivership filings (CourtListener-style Ontario/BC registries), land registry.
- Existing literature: Urbanation (above); CMHC Supply Report; BoC SAP 2026-52 (supply response weaker than demand to rate shocks).
- Evidence against / competing: Rental starts rose as condo starts fell (substitution, not total collapse; national starts +6% in 2025); prior presale boom 2016–21 coincided with low rates AND speculative demand, so decline may be reversion from bubble rather than structural barrier; price falls (~25% below presale in Chloe) suggest earlier prices were unsustainable, not that supply is constrained by cost alone.
- Likely identification: Event-study/DiD on rate-sensitivity across project types (presale-reliant vs. rental/MLI-financed), instrument with 2022 rate-hike timing and project-stage at shock; project-level hazard model of cancellation.
- Expected difficulty: Med (private presale data, license).
- Novelty: Low–Med. Policy importance: High (completion cliff). Proposed class: B

### CONS-2 — Collapse in residential construction productivity raises real cost per unit
- Mechanism: Output per worker in residential building fell ~2%/yr 2001–2023; if real, each unit needs more labour and hours, raising costs beyond materials inflation.
- Predicted effect: Negative; cumulative -37% (press-reported) implies large real cost wedge, but magnitude on prices unmeasured.
- Unit of analysis: Firm/industry-year; CMA.
- Required variables: Value-added or physical output (units, sf) per hour; quality/mix (size, height, regulation).
- Datasets: StatCan firm-level (T2/payroll linked), Table 36-10-0480; BCPI; CMHC.
- Literature: Watt, Gu, ab Iorwerth 2026 (above). Productivity measurement debate in construction generally (deflator problems) [unverified in Canadian residential context].
- Evidence against: Measurement: real output deflated by construction price index containing quality/regulatory change; shift to complex typologies (more concrete high-rise, mandated energy/accessibility/fire codes) would show as lower "productivity" while delivering more per unit; small firms doing easy single-detached still decline, which argues against pure composition (ab Iorwerth quote) but not against code stringency; no 2023–24 residential-only figure found; the broad construction sector rose 1% 2023–24 (ISED).
- Identification: Decompose by firm size, structure type, region; exploit regional variation where growth positive (Watt et al. note some); compare with US residential construction productivity as benchmark (Goolsbee–Syverson type) [unverified].
- Difficulty: High (measurement). Novelty: Med. Policy importance: High. Class: C

### CONS-3 — Skilled-labour capacity/retirement cliff is the binding supply constraint
- Mechanism: Retiring cohort not replaced; wages rise, cost and schedule slip; capacity caps starts regardless of demand.
- Predicted effect: Negative on starts and positive on hard-cost growth; BuildForce shortfall 67,600 residential by 2034 (slide deck figure).
- Unit: Trade × province × year.
- Variables: Employment by NOC, vacancy, wage growth, apprentice completions, immigration inflows (trades).
- Datasets: BuildForce LMI, LFS, Job Vacancy and Wage Survey, IRCC Express Entry, Red Seal/apprentice data.
- Literature: BuildForce; Express Entry data (18,775 construction workers 2019–Apr 2025).
- Evidence against: Starts hit record in rental-heavy cities (Calgary, Edmonton) in 2025 despite "shortage"; cost inflation slowed to 3.0% in 2025 and condo starts fell, so labour demand weakened (BC residential employment flat to 2034); a shortage measured as unfilled forecast gaps is model-dependent; vacancy data not retrieved. Capacity may bind only in peaks (2021–22) and be slack in 2024–26.
- Identification: Province/trade shift-share with demand shocks; regional wage-vacancy elasticity; natural experiment of 2025 immigration cuts (note cuts may lower demand too).
- Difficulty: Med. Novelty: Low. Importance: High. Class: C

### CONS-4 — Viability gap: all-in cost exceeds what market rents/prices support (viability, not capacity, binds)
- Mechanism: Land + hard + soft + financing + government charges + return hurdle > value at current rents/prices; projects not feasible, so little built despite shortage of units. Residual land-value logic: land sellers hold out.
- Predicted effect: Negative on starts, shifting from high-rise ownership to subsidized/low-cost financed rental; sign clear, magnitude requires pro forma modelling.
- Unit: Representative project pro forma by CMA/typology.
- Variables: Cost per sf, achievable rents/prices, cap rates, loan rates/LTV, land price per buildable sf, charges.
- Datasets: Altus Cost Guide, CMHC rental market survey, municipal DC schedules, MLI Select assumptions, brokerage land comps (Altus, CBRE), CMHC Supply Report developer feedback.
- Literature: CMHC Spring 2026 report (feasibility strained, 49% developers negative, secondary); I did not retrieve specific pro forma shares [unverified].
- Evidence against / competing: Rents and prices are high relative to incomes, so viability falls from rates/cost rather than "under-pricing"; record rental starts show viability works under subsidy; if costs are inflated by policy (CONS-5), viability gap partially policy-made; capacity vs viability contested (see CONS-3).
- Identification: Calibrated pro forma with counterfactual removal of each cost component (accounting, not causal); causal part from discontinuities (e.g., DC changes, GST rebate timing).
- Difficulty: Med. Novelty: Low–Med. Importance: High. Class: B

### CONS-5 — Development charges and government levies as a cost wedge
- Mechanism: DCs/CBCs/parkland and fees (~20–25% of GTA condo price, Altus-BILD 2018) raise cost; charges are paid upfront, raising financing and viability thresholds; incidence may fall on land value rather than price.
- Predicted effect: Negative on starts; ~ -10–24% on dwelling cost if removed (CMHC–Altus, accounting).
- Unit: Municipality-year, project.
- Variables: DC rates, timing, project outcomes, land values.
- Datasets: Municipal DC by-laws, BILD/Altus, CMHC starts, land registry.
- Literature: Altus/BILD 2018; Vancouver CMHC remark. Incidence literature (US impact fees: e.g., Evans-Cowley et al.; Ihlanfeldt & Shaughnessy 2004) [unverified].
- Evidence against: Industry-commissioned; incidence may fall on landowners (reduces land price not output) ; DCs fund real infrastructure; 2024–25 Ontario DC cuts/freezes/ changes (Bill 23) are a test I did not look up; counted nominal share not elasticity.
- Identification: DiD on DC changes (Bill 23 Ontario 2022; Toronto/Vancouver deferrals), border discontinuity between municipalities.
- Difficulty: Med. Novelty: Low (shared with public-finance agent). Importance: High. Class: B

### CONS-6 — Tax/finance subsidies (GST rebate, MLI Select/ACLP) reshape tenure and are largest lever on viability, with uncertain additionality
- Mechanism: 100% GST rebate (~5% of cost), 50-year amortization/95% LTV insured MLI Select [terms from memory, unverified], and low-rate ACLP make rental feasible where condos are not; shifts output from condo to rental.
- Predicted effect: Positive on rental starts; ambiguous net on total (condo starts down). 2025 rental starts record in multiple CMAs; Toronto rental > condo starts.
- Unit: Project; CMA-quarter.
- Variables: Rental starts vs condo, financing program uptake, cancellation-to-rental conversions (Urbanation: 8 projects, 2,189 units).
- Datasets: CMHC starts by tenure, CMHC MLI/ACLP program data (request), CRA rebate claims.
- Literature: CMHC Supply Report; CRA guidance; no causal evaluation found.
- Evidence against: Might be substitution/time shifting not net addition; ~179,000 MLI Select homes insured 2024 includes existing buildings (not new supply) [single secondary source]; developer survey 37% optimistic only; rental projects reliant on subsidy raise fiscal cost; GST rebate also lowers rents? unknown.
- Identification: DiD/event study around Sept 2023 GST announcement and Dec 2024 MLI changes comparing rental vs condo typologies, and provinces with provincial HST rebates (ON, NS, NL).
- Difficulty: Med. Novelty: Med (few evaluations). Importance: High. Class: D

### CONS-7 — Fragmented industry structure (small firms) prevents scale and learning
- Mechanism: Two-thirds of residential employment in <20-worker firms; low capital intensity and little process innovation; high entry/exit.
- Predicted effect: Negative on productivity; yet StatCan shows small advantage for larger firms, so magnitude small.
- Unit: Firm.
- Datasets: StatCan linked data, CMHC, Canadian Business Counts.
- Literature: Watt et al. 2026 (above).
- Evidence against: Larger firms' advantage small; reallocation <5% of growth; productivity fell at all sizes (reported "smaller firms accounted"); developer concentration (not retrieved) differs from builder size.
- Identification: Firm panel, event studies of entry/exit; compare countries.
- Difficulty: Med. Novelty: Med. Importance: Med. Class: B

### CONS-8 — Industrialized construction (modular/mass timber) not scaling because demand volatility and financing block adoption
- Mechanism: High fixed cost plants need steady order books; volatile starts, code/approval fragmentation, lender unfamiliarity prevent adoption; if scaled it could save labour (mass timber "halve workers" claim vs. concrete, anecdotal).
- Predicted effect: Positive on productivity if scaled; empirical magnitude unknown.
- Unit: Project/plant.
- Datasets: CMHC Housing Supply Challenge, BC DASH, firm interviews; no national share of starts found.
- Literature: C.D. Howe 2025 report; IRPP 2024.
- Evidence against: Evidence of cost savings is thin; international cases (Sweden/Japan) mixed [unverified]; transport weight/logistics; "not always more affordable" (CMHC per IRPP).
- Identification: Procurement pilots with randomised/staggered rollout; case studies.
- Difficulty: High. Novelty: Med. Importance: Med. Class: D

### CONS-9 — Interest-rate cost-of-carry and long timelines make supply slow and policy-uncertain
- Mechanism: Approval-to-completion of years inflates holding cost; rate hikes raise carry on land and construction loans (land is financed); delays compound viability gap.
- Predicted effect: Negative; cost per year of delay unquantified here [no data retrieved].
- Unit: Project timeline.
- Datasets: CMHC Starts/Completions (time-to-complete), municipal permit data, CMHC approval timelines.
- Literature: BoC SAP 2026-35 on nonlinear rate effects on housing (abstract only); BoC SAP 2026-52 (supply less responsive).
- Evidence against: Overlap with planning agent; approval-time causes are regulatory not construction; construction duration is mostly physical.
- Identification: Rate-shock event-study on project stage; permit-queue shocks.
- Difficulty: Med. Novelty: Low. Importance: Med–High. Class: B

### CONS-10 — Land cost as residual and land-assembly/holdout constraint
- Mechanism: Land is priced off expected buildable value; zoning-scarce land + holdouts in assembly keep land price high; falling pre-sale prices should push land values down but sellers wait.
- Predicted effect: Negative on starts in short run (price-discovery lag); share of land in cost not verified.
- Unit: Parcel/transaction.
- Datasets: Land registry (Teranet, GeoWarehouse), Altus land sales, MPAC.
- Literature: [unverified] — none retrieved. Land share in total cost not found.
- Evidence against: Standard theory: land is residual to supply constraints so cause lies in zoning (planning domain); falling land values 2023–25 in GTA reported anecdotally [unverified].
- Identification: Land transaction event studies; DiD on upzoning (e.g., Ontario, BC TOA areas).
- Difficulty: High (data). Novelty: Med. Importance: High. Class: D

## Disagreements / contested points
1. Capacity vs viability: BuildForce frames labour shortfall as binding; CMHC and Urbanation evidence (record rental starts in Calgary/Edmonton, 2025 starts +6% while condo starts collapse, cost inflation falling to 3%) points to viability/financing as binding in Toronto/Vancouver. Probably regime-dependent (2021–22 capacity; 2024–26 viability); untested.
2. Productivity decline real vs measurement artifact (deflator, code, typology); StatCan figure reported variously (37.3% vs "38.5 pp").
3. Rental boom additionality: record rental starts but are they net new or tenure shift, and fiscally costly? Developer sentiment on rental feasibility negative despite subsidies.
4. National starts: C.D. Howe cites 245,000 starts "last year" vs CMHC Spring 2026 ~259,000 for 2025 (possibly different year/definition).
